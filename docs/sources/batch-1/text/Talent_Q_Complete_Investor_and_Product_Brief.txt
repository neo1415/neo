1. Executive summary
Talent Q is a proposed AI-native recruitment platform designed to solve a stubborn
problem: finding applicants has become easier than establishing who can do the work, who
actually wants the opportunity, and how to move both parties to a good hiring decision.
Employers still coordinate sourcing, screening, contact, interviews and offers across
disconnected systems. Candidates repeat the same history, receive inconsistent
information and rarely retain useful evidence from one hiring process to the next.

Talent Q would let an employer explain a hiring need in ordinary language. Q would clarify
the work to be done, translate it into a structured and reviewable hiring mandate, discover
people from lawful and permissioned sources, show what is known and unknown about
their capabilities, check mutual employment preferences, and coordinate the next steps.
On the candidate side, Q would support a reusable professional profile, practical
demonstrations of ability, structured interviews, preparation and skill growth.



   THE INVESTMENT THESIS
   Do not sell more AI-generated CV rankings. Sell a shorter, more trustworthy
   path from a genuine hiring need to a qualified, interested candidate and an
   accountable hiring process.


The first paying customers should be organizations with repeated professional hiring:
specialist recruitment agencies and small to mid-sized employers with recurring roles.
Rather than compete immediately for the entire global job market, the business would
launch a narrow role and geographic segment, prove hiring outcomes, and expand by
repeatable playbooks. It can be built as a globally configurable product without claiming
global distribution on day one.

Talent Q has no demonstrated customer traction or validated Q-Match predictive
advantage as of this brief. The opportunity is conditional: access to compliant candidate
data, credible assessment evidence, repeat buyer willingness to pay, disciplined AI service
costs, and real performance improvements against existing workflows.

2. The problem and the customer
Why recruitment remains difficult
A job posting can produce hundreds of resumes while still failing to produce a confident
hire. Employers encounter vague hiring requirements, low-signal applications, incomplete
professional profiles, nonresponsive candidates, complex scheduling and limited
assessment expertise. Job seekers encounter low-visibility applications, repetitive forms,
opaque decisions and expensive or irrelevant preparation.

In a 2026 survey of 2,094 US HR professionals, SHRM reported that 68% found full-time
recruitment difficult, and 41% were training existing employees for hard-to-fill roles. This is
survey evidence of the problem, not proof that Talent Q can solve it. [03]

Greenhouse reported in October 2026 that 81% of surveyed US candidates had been
rejected without speaking to anyone at the company, while 45% of respondents who had
interviewed reported employer ghosting. The multi-country survey included more than
2,800 job seekers, but the quoted percentages refer to the reported US groups. [04]


The four primary customers
Occasional employer. A founder or manager who hires infrequently and needs help
defining a role, assessing people and keeping the process moving without employing a full
recruitment team.

Recurring employer. An HR or talent-acquisition team hiring multiple roles each quarter,
seeking better candidate evidence and less administrative work without necessarily
replacing its ATS.

Recruitment agency. A recruiter managing multiple clients and candidates. The product
must respect client separation, authorization to represent candidates, placement
attribution and repeat sourcing workflows.

Candidate. A graduate, experienced employee, career switcher or specialist who wants
opportunities appropriate to their abilities, pay expectations, location and career ambitions.


The actual customer outcome
For employers, the unit of value is not a profile found or a message sent. It is the time and
expense required to reach a defensible hiring decision. For candidates, the value is being
considered for appropriate work, having meaningful control over their information, and
building professional evidence that helps over time.

3. The solution: how Talent Q works
The employer journey, in ordinary language
Step 1. Explain the job
A founder says: “We need a backend engineer who can stop our API failures and improve
database performance. We can pay up to $2,000 per month, and they must overlap with
our working hours.” Q does not begin blindly searching. It asks which outcomes matter,
what is mandatory, which skills are negotiable, whether remote work is possible, and who
can make the hiring decision.

Step 2. Build a real hiring mandate
Q writes a clear brief with responsibilities, competencies, budget, working conditions,
assessment criteria and approval boundaries. The employer confirms it. If the combination
looks unrealistic, Q explains the trade-offs and offers options: change budget, relax
location, train a junior hire or narrow requirements.

Step 3. Find people and show the evidence
Q searches authorized candidate profiles, employer-approved historical applicants,
compatible partners and permitted public professional information. It shows the strongest
current matches, indicating where evidence comes from and when it was last confirmed.
No verified candidate found is a legitimate result; Q must not fabricate a shortlist.

Step 4. Confirm whether interest is mutual
Before arranging interviews, Q checks whether the candidate is open to the role, the
compensation range is viable, location and working patterns fit, and key requirements are
confirmed. Recruiters can approve outreach and review communications. People who opt
out should not receive repeated messages.

Step 5. Assess and decide
Q can run a structured text or voice screening, coordinate a specialist practical test, collect
interviewer feedback and present a comparison tied to the employer’s original rubric. A
human makes the consequential hiring decision. Q helps communicate the outcome,
schedule next steps and keep records current.

The candidate journey
The candidate uploads a CV or starts by talking to Q. Q creates a private professional
profile from information the candidate approves, asks about work preferences and
supports optional evidence such as credentials, portfolios and assessments. The candidate
can see relevant opportunities, decide which information to share, complete appropriate
interviews, track status where the employer participates, and receive meaningful feedback
or learning suggestions when permitted.

Talent Q is not a generic course marketplace. Practice and learning exist because they close
demonstrated competency gaps and improve candidates’ ability to secure suitable work.



   PRODUCT BOUNDARY
   A single product for matching and progressing talent. Interview practice,
   learning, specialist assessments and integrations are supporting journeys,
   not reasons to turn Talent Q into a separate LMS, cyber range, or all-purpose
   HR suite.

6. Q-Match: proposed matching and evidence
method
The core matching engine should be a set of transparent services, not one large language
model prompted to “choose the best candidate.” Models can interpret language and explain
findings, but access controls, source permissions, mandatory conditions, scoring and state
transitions should be checked by deterministic code.

Step one: define the role and legitimate constraints
A mandate contains responsibilities, observable skills, weights, employment conditions,
approved criteria and a rubric version. The system checks confirmed legal and role-relevant
requirements before ranking. Missing data is marked “unverified,” not treated as evidence
of failure. Age, sex, ethnicity, disability, inferred personality and social-media lifestyle must
not become routine score inputs; any lawful occupational exception requires case-by-case
legal review.


Step two: calculate employer competency fit
For candidate i and requirement j, let w(j) be the requirement weight (all weights sum to 1),
s(i,j) the demonstrated skill score from 0 to 1, c(i,j) confidence in that evidence from 0 to 1,
and m(j) a pre-established neutral prior for uncertain evidence.



   PROPOSED BASELINE FORMULA
   Employer fit F(i) = 100 × SUM over requirements j of w(j) × [ c(i,j) × s(i,j) + (1 −
   c(i,j)) × m(j) ].


Separately report evidence coverage C(i) = SUM w(j) × c(i,j). High fit with low coverage is a
warning to collect more evidence, not an instruction to reject the candidate. Source
timestamps, contradictions and assessment quality must influence confidence under
pre-specified rules.


Step three: measure candidate-side compatibility
Build a distinct, candidate-controlled preference score A(i) covering compensation,
location, working pattern, role ambitions and availability. Each dimension receives an
explicit nonnegative weight. If preferences are unknown, present the match as provisional
and ask the person instead of inventing a preference.


Step four: combine the two sides

   PROPOSED MUTUAL INDEX
   Mutual index M(i) = square root of [ F(i) × A(i) ], for candidates who satisfy
   confirmed legitimate eligibility conditions. This is a ranking heuristic, not a
   probability of hiring success.

Illustrative comparison: Candidate A has employer fit 82 and candidate compatibility 90,
giving a mutual index of about 85.9. Candidate B has employer fit 93 and compatibility 40,
giving about 61.0. Candidate A is the stronger initial mutual fit, although interviews and
further checks could change the judgment.

A shortlist should also account for evidence diversity, source quality, interview capacity
and search coverage. Showing the top 15 numerical scores without checking what each
number represents would be a design failure.


Step five: validate before making predictive claims
The weights, priors and thresholds start as design parameters. They must be tested against
structured recruiter assessments, candidate interest outcomes and subsequent hiring
results. When sufficient permissioned data exist, later models may estimate probabilities
such as interview acceptance or offer acceptance. Those probabilities require
out-of-sample calibration, data-leakage controls, segment-level fairness testing and
monitoring. Do not present a fit index as “an 86% chance of success.”


What could actually become proprietary
The formula itself is easy to copy. Defensibility would instead come from a role-specific
competency taxonomy, high-quality candidate evidence, permissioned outcome data,
standardized evaluation methods, well-calibrated ranking, and trustworthy orchestration
across real employers and candidates. These do not yet exist at scale for Talent Q.



7. Technical architecture, AI agents and speed
The system in five layers
1. Experience. Web/mobile interfaces for employers, agencies and candidates; accessible
text and voice interactions, clear state, human decisions and transparent controls.

2. Governed Q orchestration. Intent understanding, memory, tool planning, permission
checks, task execution, session continuity, voice and provider routing. Q is the shared brain
inside Talent Q; no investor-facing dependency on another brand.

3. Recruitment domain services. Hiring mandates, evidence store, candidate profiles,
consent, search, Q-Match, outreach, interviews, assessments, calendar coordination and
pipeline.

4. Data and integrations. Transactional database, search indexes, document storage, event
log, vector retrieval, approved ATS, messaging, licensed data and assessment providers.

5. Assurance. Role permissions, tenant isolation, auditing, data rights, budget caps, human

The agent model
A recruiter can delegate sourcing, shortlist refreshes, interview coordination or follow-ups
to an agent for a defined mandate. Every agent action has a scope, permitted tool list,
explicit spend and communication limits, time to live, records and a human escalation rule.
Agents may propose a rejection, but they should not independently make a consequential
final hiring decision.


Interviews and practical assessments
Use a provider-neutral adapter for video avatars and live voice, so the user can choose
low-cost text, audio or premium video when appropriate. A technical interview does not
always need an avatar. For cybersecurity, a controlled synthetic alert investigation is a
sensible starting exercise; for software engineering, use a reputable coding environment;
for sales, roleplay can measure job-specific behaviours. Full cyber ranges or IDEs can be
integrated rather than rebuilt initially.

Where the opportunity could be
 • Better role understanding for non-experts: founders often need to describe a business
   outcome before they know what title to hire.

 • Evidence portability: candidates should not have to demonstrate the same skill from
   scratch when validated, consented evidence can be reused appropriately.

 • Mutual suitability early: compensation, working conditions and interest should be
   discussed before costly interviews.

 • Actionable candidate status: participating employers can commit to review windows
   and more reliable communication.

 • Practical assessment orchestration: the platform selects or connects the correct tools
   without demanding every employer learn a specialist testing product.


Geographic strategy: global design, selective launch
A global product must accommodate different currencies, languages, employment rules,
data locations and candidate expectations. The initial commercial effort should compare
Nigerian or West African professional-role employers, international specialist agencies and
English-speaking mid-market teams. The winner is the segment with lawful data access,
repeat mandates, reachable buyers, reasonable acquisition cost and strong retention, not
simply the biggest country.

9. Market entry and distribution plan
The first commercial wedge
Begin with a managed software-assisted pilot for specialist agencies and employers
handling recurring professional hiring. Concentrate on two or three role families, such as
business development, technical support and software engineering. This reduces the
assessment design space and allows a clear comparison with existing recruitment
workflows.


How the first customers are acquired
Design partners. Recruit ten to fifteen employers or agencies for detailed intake interviews,
secure a small set of real hiring mandates and establish their existing costs and conversion
rates.

Proof-of-value pilots. Deliver permissioned shortlists with source and interest verification,
track recruiter hours and candidate response, then charge at least a modest pilot fee.

Repeatability. Standardize successful role rubrics, outreach approvals, evidence criteria and
support playbooks. Measure renewals and repeated usage.

Partner distribution. Evaluate niche recruiters, HR consultancies, professional
communities, training organizations and approved ATS partners, with consent and
representation terms.

Geographic expansion. Open a new country only after local legal review, source contracts,
currency/payment handling, support capability and buyer economics are credible.


The first measurable funnel
One scenario: 8,000 reachable organizations × 35% contactable × 12% response × 35%
meeting conversion × 25% paid conversion gives roughly 29 paying accounts. This is an
illustrative funnel to falsify, not a customer forecast. Track every stage in a CRM and
replace all percentages with observed cohort data.


What constitutes product-market progress
A good sign is not large free signup volume. It is repeat paid mandates, organizations
renewing for a second cycle, recruiters spending materially less time per acceptable
shortlist, candidates willing to participate without coercion, and paid accounts whose direct
delivery costs stay bounded.

USD 256 implies approximately 4.3 months of gross-contribution payback. At an assumed
monthly logo churn of 5%, simple undiscounted contribution LTV would be USD 5,120, or
4.7 times CAC. But 5% monthly churn corresponds to roughly 46% of accounts lost
annually before replacement and should not be treated as a healthy long-term target.

Operational dashboards must show paid acquisition cost, fully loaded acquisition payback,
source and interview cost per mandate, support effort, monthly churn, expansion revenue,
candidate response, shortlist-to-interview conversion, interview-to-offer conversion and
time-to-hire.

13. Risks, legal obligations and failure scenarios
Dependence on external talent data
Risk: LinkedIn does not permit unauthorized scraping or automation. Recruiter
subscriptions are not blanket data-licensing agreements. [12] Response: Use
candidate-owned profiles, employer records with appropriate permissions, licensed
providers and approved connectors; test data economics.

Algorithmic hiring and bias
Risk: Hiring tools can affect livelihoods and can reproduce historical discrimination.
Response: Use job-related criteria, human review, documented rubrics, accessibility, impact
evaluations and challenge/correction routes.

Privacy and regulatory fragmentation
Risk: European recruitment-selection AI appears in AI Act Annex III as a high-risk category;
the EU currently lists December 2027 as the application date for those high-risk
obligations. [13][14] Response: Obtain qualified local advice, country-specific policies,
consent records, traceability, incident response and audited deployment practices.

Automation replacing actual recruiter judgment
Risk: Companies may over-rely on an apparently authoritative score or transcript.
Response: Scores are provisional aids; decisions require a legitimate human reviewer and
source evidence.

Candidate trust
Risk: Unwanted outreach, social-media investigation and private interview reuse could
drive users away. Response: Permissioned communication, minimized collection, visible
sharing controls, deletion/correction, clear alternatives to video.

Unit costs and provider dependency
Risk: Sourcing licenses, voice sessions and premium video can overwhelm fixed
subscription revenue. Response: Budget gates, usage caps, adapter architecture, metered
add-ons, provider switching and full-bill monitoring.

Marketplace cold start
Risk: Employers want qualified candidates before paying; candidates want real jobs before
joining. Response: Enter through paying employers and authorized existing talent pools;
build candidate network from genuine demand.

Sales cycles and retention
Risk: Recruitment freezes or a one-time role can eliminate ongoing spend. Response:
Target repeated hiring workflows and agencies, test mandates alongside subscriptions,
measure cohort retention.

Weak predictive validity
Risk: Q-Match could perform no better than conventional screening. Response: Blind

14. Development roadmap, evidence gates and
operating milestones
Phase 0: technical and demand validation
Audit Q reuse boundaries and data isolation; interview prospective employers and
candidates; confirm sourcing rights; define two role families, standard job rubrics and a
measurable conventional recruiting baseline. No claim of proprietary algorithm

Phase 1: paid pilot, minimum complete journey
Build candidate and employer accounts, role intake, approved candidate import, retrieval,
Q-Match baseline, reviewable shortlists, consented outreach, simple pipeline and recruiter
dashboard. Run actual mandates end to end, including failures and refusals. The product is
useful only if a qualified interested candidate can reach a recruiter decision.


Phase 2: structured interviews and candidate growth
Add text/voice screens, practical assessment orchestration, interview scheduling,
candidate-owned reusable evidence, targeted practice and compliant feedback. Validate
assessment reliability and candidate experience, particularly on older devices and weak
